AI in everyday IT Franklin Waggoner

Speaker notes

At Block, I initiated two projects to make AI more useful in everyday IT work.

For the Jira MCP, I reproduced an inconsistent user mention bug, traced it to account ID parsing, implemented the fix, and tested it with teammates. AI Security reviewed and deployed the change. I then contributed the improvement to the open source Jira MCP project, where the maintainer merged my work.

For the IT Support Knowledge Base, I obtained an export of about 500 articles, brought them into GitHub as Markdown, built a documentation site, and tested search and updates through Slack and Goose. My manager and his manager supported the prototype. IT Operations ultimately selected Glean for broader knowledge ingestion.

Speaker notes

Block built Goose, and our team was using it with a community Jira MCP before official vendor MCP support was available in our workflow. The MCP carried out Jira actions on behalf of the agent.

User mentions behaved inconsistently. Some people appeared as real Jira mentions, while other attempts became plain text. I encountered this myself and used a workaround.

IT Support then told me this bug was one reason some coworkers avoided the AI workflow. That made the problem more important to solve. I took ownership of reproducing the failure, finding its cause, and implementing a fix.

Speaker notes

I worked with one teammate whose mentions worked and another whose mentions failed. I sent test messages, compared the outcomes, and narrowed the difference to the account identifier.

The pattern aligned with our Jira Cloud migration. Earlier accounts could be mentioned. Employees who joined after that migration had a newer account ID format containing a colon, and that format was being misparsed by our Jira MCP.

Once I had a reproducible difference between the two inputs, I inspected the mention parsing logic. The colon gave me a specific character to trace through the code.

Speaker notes

The mention parser used a regular expression to recognize user identifiers. Its allowed characters excluded the colon present in the affected Jira Cloud account IDs. Those identifiers fell through to plain text.

I added the colon to the character set and included other missing characters. The core fix was a focused change to the matching pattern.

The example on this slide shows why it worked: including the colon allows the full identifier to match. I retested both account formats with the same teammates. The earlier format continued to work, and the previously failing format produced a Jira mention.

Speaker notes

I changed the parser and retested the mention behavior with the teammates involved in the investigation. I checked that both the previously working account format and the affected Jira Cloud format produced the expected mentions.

I brought the tested change to AI Security, who reviewed it and deployed it at Block. The previously failing mentions worked in production.

I had taken a frustrating tool behavior through reproduction, diagnosis, implementation, and validation. The result removed a specific bug that IT Support had identified as a barrier to using the workflow.

Speaker notes

After the internal fix, I contributed a related change to the open source Jira MCP project using my personal GitHub account. The maintainer merged it, making this my first accepted open source contribution.

Pull request #12 in George5562/Jira-MCP-Server was merged on September 8, 2025. The public contribution expands Jira Cloud mention parsing and converts the result into Atlassian Document Format mention nodes. The pattern shown here captures the identifier value, including a colon within it.

This was a meaningful way to give back to a tool our team was already benefiting from. It also made me more attentive to opportunities to contribute fixes to other open source tools.

Speaker notes

The second project focused on the IT Support Knowledge Base. Notion’s available MCP and API access created friction: I had to export PDFs, copy text, or use screenshots to provide context to an AI agent. The integration available to us also granted broad access across shared content. We needed it to honor each reader’s permissions.

Our knowledge management team had built a well curated Notion experience, with drafts reviewed before publication. I wanted to carry that curation into an AI native workflow.

Cash App and Goose engineering guides showed what GitHub hosted documentation could look like. Engineers were already connecting Slack and AI tools to their work. I saw an opportunity to bring the IT Support Knowledge Base into that same pattern.

Speaker notes

I proposed GitHub as the home for the IT Support Knowledge Base, with Markdown as the source and a documentation site for readers. Goose would let us find information and describe article changes from Slack or the Goose app.

Our existing knowledge process already involved drafting an article, reviewing it, and approving it for publication. I mapped that process to a proposed pull request workflow. A diff would show the change, and the knowledge management team would continue to approve or refine the content.

I tested search and article updates in the prototype. The review and publishing flow shown here was the model I proposed for extending it.

GitHub was already a platform Block depended on. That made it a practical place to explore an AI native knowledge workflow alongside the engineering documentation that inspired the project.

Speaker notes

The IT Support Knowledge Base contained about 500 articles. Obtaining the export was the first practical obstacle, so I worked directly with Notion support to get the source material.

I brought the exported articles into GitHub as Markdown and built a Docusaurus style documentation site around them. The prototype used our actual support content and gave me a working foundation for testing the AI workflow.

I began functional testing with a focused selection of articles covering common IT tasks. That let me check whether Goose could search the content and apply useful updates from the places we already worked.

Speaker notes

I tested selected articles through the Goose bot in Slack and in Goose itself. I focused on two everyday actions: finding the information needed to answer a support question and updating an article when a step changed.

For search, I asked Goose to find relevant information and checked the content it returned. For updates, I described a change and checked the resulting Markdown. Printer setup and WiFi guidance were useful examples of familiar support tasks.

Both behaviors worked well on the selected content. The prototype demonstrated a useful connection between our knowledge source and the conversations where support work happened.

Speaker notes

My manager liked the prototype, and we presented it to his manager, who also supported the idea. I gave leadership a working example of how the IT Support Knowledge Base could be searched and updated through Goose.

The knowledge management team had invested in the existing experience. Their review and curation remained central to the workflow I proposed.

IT Operations ultimately selected Glean. It supported a broader range of documentation sources and could stay current as people updated the original content. The GitHub work remained a tested prototype, while Glean became the direction for knowledge ingestion.

The prototype demonstrated another way to connect AI with support knowledge and gave the team a concrete alternative to evaluate.

Speaker notes

The Jira MCP project resulted in a tested fix deployed at Block and an accepted open source contribution. The knowledge project resulted in a working GitHub prototype that I demonstrated to leadership.

In both projects, I initiated the work, investigated the practical problem, built an improvement, and tested the behavior. I also worked with teammates, AI Security, vendor support, and leadership to move each idea forward.

The shared lesson is to make the problem specific enough to investigate and the result concrete enough to test. A small parsing change and a working knowledge prototype both started with something I wanted to make better in daily IT work.
